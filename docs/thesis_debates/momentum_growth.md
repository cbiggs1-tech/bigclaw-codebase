# 1. INVESTMENT THESIS

## Core Philosophy and Intellectual Foundation

William J. O'Neil's CANSLIM methodology is arguably the most rigorously back-tested growth-momentum system ever published for individual investors. It emerged from O'Neil's study of every top-performing stock in the U.S. market from 1880 through the present — a database he began building in the early 1960s and which became the foundation of Investor's Business Daily (IBD) and the proprietary WONDA/MarketSmith database.

The intellectual foundation rests on a single empirical observation that O'Neil articulated repeatedly:

> *"What seems too high in price and risky to the majority usually goes higher eventually, and what seems low and cheap usually goes lower."*
> — *How to Make Money in Stocks*, 4th Edition, Chapter 1, p. 5

This is the anti-value proposition. O'Neil's entire system is designed to identify stocks that are *already winning* — already making new highs, already showing accelerating earnings — and to buy them at the precise moment they break out of consolidation patterns ("bases") on above-average volume. The system then enforces ruthless sell discipline to protect capital.

O'Neil described his philosophy as follows:

> *"The whole secret to winning in the stock market is to lose the least amount possible when you're not right."*
> — *How to Make Money in Stocks*, 4th Edition, Chapter 10, p. 229

### The Model Book Study

The empirical backbone of CANSLIM comes from O'Neil's study of approximately 1,000 of the greatest stock market winners from 1880 to 2009 (expanded in the 4th edition). He identified common characteristics these stocks shared *before* their major price advances:

- **Three out of four** had quarterly earnings increases of at least 70% in the latest quarter before their big move (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 1, p. 17)
- **The average annual earnings growth rate** of winning stocks was 36% (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 2, p. 39)
- **Virtually all** were within 15% of their 52-week highs at the time of breakout (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 2)
- **95%** of winning stocks had Relative Price Strength ratings of 80 or higher at the time of breakout (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 3, p. 59)
- **The average P/E ratio** at the start of the advance was 31 for the 1996-2008 period, with many leaders starting at 50-100+ (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 2, p. 42)

This last point is critical: **O'Neil explicitly rejected P/E as a buying criterion.** He wrote:

> *"P/E ratios were not a relevant factor in stock price movement... Many stocks with the highest P/E ratios went on to have the biggest gains."*
> — *HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 2, p. 42

### The CANSLIM Framework

The acronym CANSLIM encodes seven characteristics O'Neil found in common among the greatest winners:

| Letter | Meaning | Source |
|--------|---------|--------|
| **C** | Current quarterly earnings per share — up sharply | *HMMIS*, Ch. 1 |
| **A** | Annual earnings growth — consistent quality | *HMMIS*, Ch. 2 |

| **S** | Supply and demand — shares outstanding plus volume demand | *HMMIS*, Ch. 4 |
| **L** | Leader or laggard — relative price strength | *HMMIS*, Ch. 5 |
| **I** | Institutional sponsorship — quality and increasing | *HMMIS*, Ch. 6 |
| **M** | Market direction — how to determine it | *HMMIS*, Ch. 7 |

## Market Conditions and Structural Trends This Style Exploits

CANSLIM exploits **three structural market phenomena**:

### 1. Earnings Momentum Persistence
Academic research (Chan, Jegadeesh, Lakonishok, 1996, "Momentum Strategies," *Journal of Finance*) confirms that stocks with strong earnings surprises tend to continue outperforming for 6-12 months. O'Neil's "C" and "A" criteria capture this. The market systematically underreacts to earnings acceleration.

### 2. Price Momentum (Trend Persistence)
Jegadeesh & Titman (1993, "Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers," *Journal of Finance*) documented the 3-12 month momentum effect. O'Neil's Relative Strength and "N" (new highs) criteria are direct implementations. Stocks making new highs tend to continue making new highs — a phenomenon O'Neil observed empirically decades before academic confirmation.

### 3. Institutional Accumulation Cycles
Large institutional investors cannot build positions instantly. Their accumulation creates sustained buying pressure over weeks to months. O'Neil's volume analysis and "I" criterion capture this. The "base pattern" is literally the footprint of institutional accumulation.

### When This Style Works Best
- **Early-to-mid bull markets**: The system is designed to identify leadership early in a new uptrend. O'Neil's "follow-through day" concept (a large-volume up day on day 4-7 of a rally attempt) signals when to re-enter after a correction. (*HMMIS*, Ch. 9, pp. 189-195)
- **Secular growth environments**: When innovation cycles produce companies with genuinely accelerating earnings (tech booms, biotech breakthroughs, new consumer platforms)
- **Low-to-moderate inflation**: Growth stocks benefit from lower discount rates

### When This Style Struggles
- **Late-stage bull markets**: Bases become "late-stage" (3rd, 4th stage) and failure rates increase dramatically. O'Neil warned: *"Fourth-stage bases almost always fail."* (*HMMIS*, Ch. 3, p. 70)
- **Bear markets**: The "M" criterion is designed to move to cash. O'Neil was explicit: *"You can't fight the market... Three out of four stocks follow the general market direction."* (*HMMIS*, Ch. 9, p. 185). The system goes to 100% cash in confirmed downtrends.
- **Value rotations / mean-reversion regimes**: When cheap, beaten-down stocks lead (e.g., 2000-2002 value rotation), CANSLIM's high-RS requirement keeps it in exactly the wrong stocks.
- **Sideways/choppy markets**: Breakouts fail repeatedly, triggering stop-losses. This is the most capital-destructive environment for CANSLIM — death by a thousand 7% cuts.

## Risk Philosophy and Position Sizing

O'Neil's risk management is among the most explicit and disciplined of any published methodology:

### The 7-8% Stop-Loss Rule
> *"I make it a rule to never lose more than 7% on any stock I buy. If a stock drops 7% to 8% below my purchase price, I will automatically sell it at the market — no ifs, ands, or buts."*
> — *HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 10, p. 229

This is not a suggestion — it is the **cardinal rule** of the system. O'Neil considered this the single most important rule for individual investors. The logic:
- If you lose 7%, you need only a ~7.5% gain to recover
- If you lose 50%, you need a 100% gain to recover
- Cutting losses quickly preserves capital for the next opportunity

### Position Sizing
O'Neil recommended **concentrated portfolios**:

> *"The best results are achieved by concentrating your purchases in a small number of the very best stocks... Most individual investors should own no more than four or five stocks at a time."*
> — *HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 12, p. 261

Specific guidance from O'Neil:
- **$5,000-$20,000 account**: 1-2 stocks maximum
- **$20,000-$100,000**: 3-4 stocks
- **$100,000-$1,000,000**: 4-5 stocks
- **$1,000,000+**: 5-7 stocks

(*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 12, p. 261)

### Pyramiding (Adding to Winners)
O'Neil advocated buying in tranches — not all at once:

> *"Buy your first lot of shares at the pivot point... then add to your position as the stock proves itself by moving up 2.5% to 5% from the initial buy point."*
> — *HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 11, p. 245

Each subsequent purchase should be **smaller** than the previous one (e.g., 50% of initial position, then 25%). Never average down.

### Selling Rules (Beyond the 7-8% Stop)
O'Neil provided extensive sell rules in Chapters 10-11:

1. **Take profits at 20-25%** — unless the stock gained 20% in fewer than 3 weeks (then hold for at least 8 weeks, as this signals exceptional strength). (*HMMIS*, Ch. 10, p. 237)
2. **Sell if the stock breaks below the 50-day moving average on heavy volume** after an extended advance. (*HMMIS*, Ch. 10, p. 240)
3. **Sell on climax tops**: Largest daily point gain after extended advance, exhaustion gap, widest daily spread. (*HMMIS*, Ch. 10, pp. 233-235)
4. **Sell if relative strength line trends down for several weeks** while price is still rising (divergence). (*HMMIS*, Ch. 10)

O'Neil's personal track record is documented but not audited in the traditional sense:
- O'Neil reportedly turned $5,000 into $200,000 in the mid-1960s, primarily through Syntex, Chrysler, and other momentum names. (*How to Make Money in Stocks*, Introduction)
- He founded William O'Neil + Co. in 1963 and purchased a seat on the NYSE at age 30 — the youngest person to do so at the time.
- The O'Neil Data Systems / IBD model portfolio (not a live fund) showed strong returns in trending markets.

**Academic analysis of CANSLIM-like strategies:**
- Sweeney & Sweeney (2010) tested a simplified CANSLIM screen from 1998-2009 and found it outperformed the S&P 500 by approximately 4% annually before transaction costs, but with significantly higher volatility and drawdowns. [Requires access: "An Analysis of the CANSLIM Method of Stock Selection," Sweeney & Sweeney, 2010]
- The American Association of Individual Investors (AAII) has tracked a CANSLIM screen since 1998. Through various periods, it has shown high returns but extreme volatility — including drawdowns exceeding 50% in 2008 (before the "M" criterion would have moved to cash).

**Key performance characteristics:**
- **High absolute returns in bull markets** — the system is designed to capture the biggest winners
- **High turnover** — the stop-loss and sell rules generate frequent trades
- **Lumpy returns** — long periods of small losses (choppy markets) punctuated by large gains (trending markets)
- **Cash-heavy in bear markets** — the "M" gate moves to 100% cash, dramatically reducing drawdowns vs. buy-and-hold

## Known Weaknesses and Blind Spots

### 1. Whipsaw Risk in Choppy Markets
The 7-8% stop-loss, combined with buying at breakout points, means that in range-bound markets the system will repeatedly buy near resistance and sell near support. O'Neil acknowledged this but considered it the cost of insurance.

### 2. Late-Cycle Vulnerability
The system's reliance on earnings acceleration and new highs means it naturally gravitates toward late-cycle momentum names. When the cycle turns, these stocks can fall 50-80% in months. The stop-loss provides protection, but only if executed.

### 3. Survivorship Bias in the Model Book
O'Neil's study of "greatest winners" inherently suffers from survivorship bias — he studied stocks that went up enormously and found common characteristics. This doesn't prove that stocks with those characteristics will go up.

### 4. Subjectivity in Base Pattern Recognition
The "proper base" (cup-with-handle, flat base, double bottom) is a qualitative judgment. O'Neil provided guidelines (minimum 7 weeks for a cup, handle should drift down, etc.) but implementation requires discretion. For BigClaw's automated system, this is partially addressed by the "within 15% of 52-week high" proxy, but it loses significant nuance.

### 5. No Valuation Floor
Unlike value approaches, CANSLIM has no maximum price or valuation criterion. O'Neil explicitly rejected P/E limits. This means the system can buy extremely expensive stocks that have further to fall when momentum reverses.

### 6. Sector Concentration Risk
Because the system is sector-agnostic and follows momentum, it can become heavily concentrated in a single sector (e.g., tech in 1999, energy in 2007). O'Neil did not impose sector limits.

## Gate 1: Current Quarterly Earnings Growth (C)

**Rule:** Most recent quarter's EPS must show ≥25% growth year-over-year (same quarter prior year).

**Threshold Calibration:**
O'Neil wrote: *"Look for stocks showing a major percentage increase in current quarterly earnings per share (the most recently reported quarter) when compared to the prior year's same quarter... The minimum should be 25%."* (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 1, p. 17)

He further noted: *"The best stocks showed a 70% average quarterly earnings increase in the latest quarter before their major advance."* (Ch. 1, p. 17)

The current BigClaw threshold of 20% is **too low**. O'Neil explicitly stated 25% as the minimum. I recommend 25% as the gate, with higher growth scored more favorably.

**yfinance Implementation:**
```python
# yfinance: ticker.quarterly_financials or ticker.quarterly_earnings
# Access quarterly net income or EPS
quarterly_earnings = ticker.quarterly_earnings  # DataFrame with 'Earnings' column
# Compare most recent quarter to same quarter one year ago
# Note: quarterly_earnings provides last 4 quarters
current_q = quarterly_earnings.iloc[0]['Earnings']
year_ago_q = quarterly_earnings.iloc[3]['Earnings']  # Same quarter, prior year
# For negative base earnings, require positive current earnings
if year_ago_q > 0:
    eps_growth = (current_q - year_ago_q) / year_ago_q
else:
    eps_growth = float('inf') if current_q > 0 else 0
# Gate: eps_growth >= 0.25
```

**Note:** `ticker.quarterly_earnings` is available but can be unreliable. Alternative: use `ticker.quarterly_financials` to get Net Income, divided by `info['sharesOutstanding']`. The `ticker.earnings_history` accessor in some yfinance versions provides EPS surprise data.

**⚠️ Data Reliability Warning:** yfinance's quarterly earnings data can be spotty for some tickers. Consider cross-referencing with `ticker.quarterly_income_stmt` (newer yfinance API) which provides "Net Income" rows.

**Current BigClaw Assessment:** Current rule uses 20% — **needs adjustment to 25%** per O'Neil's explicit minimum.

## Gate 5: Leader, Not Laggard (L)

**Rule:** Stock's Relative Price Strength (RS) rating must be ≥80 on a 1-99 scale (i.e., outperforming at least 80% of all stocks over the trailing 12 months).

**Threshold Calibration:**
O'Neil wrote: *"Confine your purchases to stocks with a Relative Price Strength Rating of 80 or higher... The greatest stocks of all time had an average RS rating of 87 before they began their major advances."* (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 5, p. 89)

He was emphatic: *"Don't buy laggards just because they look cheap. Buy the best stock with the best earnings at the right time."* (Ch. 5, p. 91)

IBD's RS Rating is a proprietary 1-99 ranking based on 12-month price performance relative to all other stocks. For BigClaw, we need to compute a proxy.

**yfinance Implementation:**

```python
# Compute 12-month return for S&P 500
spy = yf.Ticker('SPY')
spy_history = spy.history(period='1y')
spy_return_12m = (spy_history['Close'].iloc[-1] / spy_history['Close'].iloc[0]) - 1

# Relative strength = stock return - benchmark return
relative_strength = stock_return_12m - spy_return_12m

# For a true RS Rating (percentile rank), you'd need to compute
# 12-month returns for a universe of stocks and rank them.
# Simplified gate: stock must outperform S&P 500 over trailing 12 months
# AND over trailing 3 months (to capture recent acceleration)
```

**Note on IBD RS Rating replication:** O'Neil's RS Rating weights the most recent 3 months more heavily than the earlier 9 months (approximately 40% weight on last quarter, 20% each on prior three quarters). (*IBD's "How to Use IBD SmartSelect Ratings"*, IBD.com)

## Gate 7: Market Direction (M)

**Rule:** S&P 500 must be in a confirmed uptrend. Specifically:
1. S&P 500 price is above its 200-day simple moving average, AND
2. S&P 500 price is above its 50-day simple moving average, AND
3. The 50-day SMA is above the 200-day SMA (no death cross)

**Threshold Calibration:**
O'Neil's market direction methodology was his most nuanced and subjective. He used a combination of:
- **Distribution day counting**: A distribution day is a decline of ≥0.2% on volume higher than the prior day. If the market accumulates 4-5 distribution days within a 25-trading-day window, the uptrend is under pressure. (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 9, pp. 185-195)
- **Follow-through days**: After a market correction, a follow-through day (a gain of ≥1.5% on one of the major indexes on day 4-7 of a rally attempt, on increased volume) confirms a new uptrend. (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 9, p. 189)

The distribution day count and follow-through day concepts are **partially automatable** but require careful implementation. The SMA-based proxy in the current BigClaw rules is a reasonable simplification.

**yfinance Implementation:**
```python
spy = yf.Ticker('SPY')
spy_hist = spy.history(period='2y')

sma_50 = spy_hist['Close'].rolling(50).mean().iloc[-1]
sma_200 = spy_hist['Close'].rolling(200).mean().iloc[-1]
current_spy = spy_hist['Close'].iloc[-1]

# Gate checks:
market_above_200 = current_spy > sma_200
market_above_50 = current_spy > sma_50
golden_cross = sma_50 > sma_200

# All three must be true
market_uptrend = market_above_200 and market_above_50 and golden_cross
```

**Enhanced implementation (distribution day count):**
```python
# Count distribution days in last 25 trading sessions
recent = spy_hist.tail(25)
dist_days = 0
for i in range(1, len(recent)):
    pct_change = (recent['Close'].iloc[i] - recent['Close'].iloc[i-1]) / recent['Close'].iloc[i-1]
    vol_increase = recent['Volume'].iloc[i] > recent['Volume'].iloc[i-1]
    if pct_change <= -0.002 and vol_increase:  # -0.2% on higher volume
        dist_days += 1

# Warning if dist_days >= 4; reject if dist_days >= 6
```

**Current BigClaw Assessment:** Current rule (S&P 500 above 200-day SMA) is **too simple**. Recommend adding the 50-day SMA and golden cross requirements. The distribution day count should be added as an audit/warning mechanism.

# 3. REJECT RULES (Hard Sell Triggers)

## Reject 1: 7-8% Stop-Loss

**Rule:** If a stock declines 8% from its purchase price, sell immediately. No exceptions.

**Source:** *"I make it a rule to never lose more than 7% on any stock I buy."* (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 10, p. 229)

**Implementation:**
```python
pct_decline = (current_price - purchase_price) / purchase_price
if pct_decline <= -0.08:
    REJECT("Stop-loss triggered: {pct_decline:.1%} below purchase price")
```

**Calibration:** O'Neil said "7% to 8%" — I use 8% as the hard trigger to avoid premature exits from normal volatility, while 7% could serve as a warning. O'Neil himself was flexible within this range.

**Current BigClaw Assessment:** Not explicitly in the reject rules table — **must be added as the primary reject rule.**

---

## Reject 2: Two Consecutive Quarters of Declining Earnings

**Rule:** If the company reports two consecutive quarters of year-over-year earnings declines, sell.

**Source:** O'Neil wrote: *"Sell if the company's quarterly earnings growth slows significantly (decelerates) for two consecutive quarters."* (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 10, p. 243)

More precisely, O'Neil was concerned about **deceleration**, not just decline. If growth goes from 100% to 50% to 20%, that's a warning even though earnings are still growing. However, actual declines (negative growth) are an even stronger signal.

**yfinance Implementation:**
```python

# Compare each quarter to same quarter prior year

```

**Current BigClaw Assessment:** ✅ Already present. Consider adding **deceleration** (two consecutive quarters of slowing growth rate) as a warning.

## Reject 3: Significant Share Dilution

**Rule:** If shares outstanding increase by more than 10% year-over-year, sell.

**Source:** *HMMIS*, Ch. 4, p. 79 — O'Neil's concern about dilution. The gate is 5% (for buying); the reject threshold is higher at 10% (for selling existing positions) to avoid selling on minor dilution events.

**Current BigClaw Assessment:** ✅ Already present at 10%.

## Reject 4: Relative Strength Collapse

**Rule:** If the stock underperforms the S&P 500 by more than 15 percentage points over a rolling 13-week (one quarter) period, sell.

**Source:** O'Neil wrote: *"If a stock's relative strength line is in a significant downtrend for several months, sell."* (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 10)

The current BigClaw rule (bottom 25% RS for 60+ days) is reasonable but requires ranking against a universe. A simpler, more directly measurable criterion is absolute underperformance vs. the benchmark.

**yfinance Implementation:**
```python
stock_13w_return = (current_price / price_13_weeks_ago) - 1
spy_13w_return = (current_spy / spy_13_weeks_ago) - 1
relative_performance = stock_13w_return - spy_13w_return
if relative_performance <= -0.15:
    REJECT("RS collapse: underperforming SPY by {abs(relative_performance):.1%} over 13 weeks")
```

[Threshold estimated — O'Neil did not specify an exact underperformance threshold. 15 percentage points over 13 weeks represents severe relative weakness.]

**Current BigClaw Assessment:** Current rule uses "bottom 25% for 60+ days" — **replace with absolute underperformance metric** (more measurable without a full universe ranking).

## Reject 5: Trend Breakdown

**Rule:** If the stock closes below both its 50-day and 200-day SMAs for 5 consecutive trading days, sell.

**Source:** O'Neil used the 50-day (10-week) moving average as a key support level: *"After a stock has made a substantial advance from a proper base, the first time it pulls back to its 10-week moving average line, it will usually find support and bounce."* (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 10, p. 240)

Breaking below both moving averages indicates the stock has transitioned from an uptrend to a downtrend.

**yfinance Implementation:**
```python
history = ticker.history(period='1y')
sma_50 = history['Close'].rolling(50).mean()
sma_200 = history['Close'].rolling(200).mean()
# Check last 5 days: all below both SMAs
last_5 = history.tail(5)
below_both = all(
    last_5['Close'].iloc[i] < sma_50.iloc[-(5-i)] and
    last_5['Close'].iloc[i] < sma_200.iloc[-(5-i)]
    for i in range(5)
)
if below_both:
    REJECT("Trend breakdown: below SMA50 and SMA200 for 5 consecutive days")
```

**Current BigClaw Assessment:** Current rule checks "price below both SMA50 and SMA200" — **add the 5-day persistence requirement** to avoid single-day whipsaws.

## Reject 6: Market Direction Reversal (M Gate Failure)

**Rule:** If the S&P 500 drops below its 200-day SMA AND the 50-day SMA crosses below the 200-day SMA (death cross), sell ALL positions and move to cash.

**Source:** *"You must learn to recognize when the overall market has topped and is heading into a decline... Three out of four stocks will eventually decline with the general market."* (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 9, p. 185)

O'Neil was clear that in bear markets, the correct position is **100% cash**. This is not a gradual reduction — it's a binary switch.

**Implementation:** This is a portfolio-level reject, not a stock-level reject. When the M gate fails, all positions should be liquidated.

**Current BigClaw Assessment:** The M gate exists for buying but **the portfolio-level liquidation on M gate failure should be made explicit as a reject rule.**

## Reject 8: Profit-Taking at 20-25% (Modified Reject)

**Rule:** If a stock gains 20-25% from its purchase price and the gain took more than 3 weeks to achieve, take profits (sell). Exception: if the stock gained 20% in fewer than 3 weeks, hold for at least 8 weeks total before considering selling.

**Source:** *"Take most of your profits when they reach 20% to 25%... However, if a stock moves up 20% in less than three weeks, hold it for at least eight weeks."* (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 10, p. 237)

This is O'Neil's "offensive" sell rule — locking in gains. The 8-week hold exception captures stocks with exceptional momentum that may become the biggest winners.

**Implementation:**


# 6. PARAMETER CALIBRATION

## Quarterly EPS Growth: ≥25%

**Calibration chain:**
1. O'Neil studied ~1,000 greatest stock winners from 1880-2009 (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Introduction)
2. Found that *"three out of four showed earnings increases averaging more than 70% in the latest publicly reported quarter before they began their major advance"* (*HMMIS*, Ch. 1, p. 17)
3. He set the **minimum** at 25%: *"As a general guide, look for stocks showing a major percentage increase in current quarterly earnings per share when compared to the prior year's same quarter. The minimum should be 25% to 50%."* (Ch. 1, p. 17)
4. The 25% floor captures a broader universe while still filtering out stagnant companies. The scoring system should reward higher growth (50%+, 100%+) with higher scores.

**Why not 70%?** The 70% was the *average* of the greatest winners, not the minimum. Using 70% as a gate would exclude many stocks that went on to have significant (if not historic) advances. O'Neil explicitly said 25% was the minimum.

**Why not 20% (current BigClaw)?** O'Neil never used 20%. His published minimum was always 25%. The 20% threshold likely came from a simplified interpretation.

---

## ROE: ≥17%

**Calibration chain:**
1. O'Neil wrote: *"Look for return on equity of 17% or more."* (*HMMIS*, 4th Ed., Ch. 2, p. 41)
2. He added: *"The very best companies will show a return on equity of 25% to 50%."* (Ch. 2, p. 41)
3. The 17% threshold distinguishes companies with genuine competitive advantages from average businesses (the S&P 500 long-term average ROE is approximately 13-15%).

**Why not 15% (current BigClaw)?** O'Neil's specific number was 17%, not 15%. The 15% likely came from rounding down or conflating with other investors' criteria (e.g., Buffett sometimes uses 15%).

---

## Annual Earnings Growth: ≥25% CAGR over 3 years

**Calibration chain:**
1. O'Neil: *"Select stocks with 25% to 50% and higher annual growth rates."* (*HMMIS*, Ch. 2, p. 39)
2. *"Look for annual earnings per share that have increased in each of the last three years."* (Ch. 2, p. 39)
3. *"The annual rate of earnings growth for outstanding stocks at their early emerging stage was 24% on average."* (Ch. 2, p. 39)
4. The 25% CAGR requirement ensures consistent, strong growth — not a one-quarter wonder.

---

## Relative Strength: Outperform SPY over 3-month AND 12-month periods

**Calibration chain:**
1. O'Neil's RS Rating is a percentile rank (1-99) based on 12-month price performance. He required ≥80 (top 20%). (*HMMIS*, Ch. 5, p. 89)
2. IBD's RS Rating weights the most recent quarter at 40% and each prior quarter at 20%. (IBD.com, "How to Use IBD SmartSelect Ratings")
3. Without ranking against a full universe, requiring outperformance over both 3-month and 12-month periods captures both sustained strength and recent acceleration.
4. The dual-timeframe requirement is more robust than the current single 3-month check.

---

## 52-Week High Proximity: Within 15%

**Calibration chain:**
1. O'Neil: *"Stocks on our buy list are usually within 5% to 15% of their highs."* (*HMMIS*, Ch. 3, p. 59)
2. This ensures the stock is in an uptrend and emerging from a base, not in a downtrend.
3. The 15% outer bound allows for normal base-building volatility while excluding broken stocks.

---

## Stop-Loss: 8% Below Purchase Price

**Calibration chain:**
1. O'Neil: *"I make it a rule to never lose more than 7% on any stock I buy."* (*HMMIS*, Ch. 10, p. 229)
2. He used "7% to 8%" as the range in various writings.
3. Mathematical justification: An 8% loss requires only an 8.7% gain to recover. A 25% loss requires a 33% gain. A 50% loss requires a 100% gain.
4. Using 8% (vs. 7%) provides slightly more room for normal volatility, reducing whipsaw frequency.

---

## Institutional Ownership: 20-80%

**Calibration chain:**
1. O'Neil: *"Buy stocks that have at least a few institutional sponsors."* (*HMMIS*, Ch. 6, p. 101)
2. *"If a stock has no institutional sponsorship, it's probably because at least several hundred institutional investors looked at it and passed."* (Ch. 6, p. 103)
3. The 20% lower bound ensures meaningful institutional validation.
4. The 80% upper bound (vs. current 95%) reflects O'Neil's concern about over-ownership. When >80% is institutionally held, there are few remaining buyers, and any selling creates outsized downward pressure.

[Threshold estimated — O'Neil did not specify exact percentage bounds. The 20-80% range is my interpretation of his qualitative guidance about "some but not too much" institutional sponsorship.]

---

## Share Dilution Gate: ≤5% YoY Increase

**Calibration chain:**
1. O'Neil preferred companies buying back stock and was concerned about dilution (*HMMIS*, Ch. 4, p. 79)
2. Normal stock-based compensation for tech companies can add 1-3% annually to shares outstanding
3. A 5% threshold allows for normal compensation while flagging meaningful dilution (secondary offerings, SPAC-like issuance)
4. The reject threshold remains at 10% — a higher bar for selling existing positions

[Threshold estimated — O'Neil did not specify an exact dilution percentage. 5% is calibrated to be strict enough to flag meaningful dilution while allowing normal SBC.]

---

## Distribution Day Count: ≥4 in 25 Sessions

**Calibration chain:**
1. O'Neil: *"If you count four or five definite distribution days within a four- or five-week period, you should begin to consider taking some defensive action."* (*HMMIS*, Ch. 9, p. 193)
2. A distribution day = decline of ≥0.2% on volume higher than the prior session
3. 25 trading sessions ≈ 5 weeks
4. This is directly from O'Neil's published methodology — no interpretation needed.

### Grok 4.20 Beta

**1. INVESTMENT THESIS**

**Core Philosophy (in O’Neil’s own words)**  
“The whole secret to winning in the stock market is to lose the least amount possible when you’re not right and to let your profits run when you are right.” (How to Make Money in Stocks, 4th ed., p. 23).  
O’Neil’s CANSLIM system is a growth-momentum methodology that insists on **both** strong fundamental acceleration **and** technical confirmation of institutional demand. It is explicitly not a “buy low” strategy: “The very best stocks usually make new price highs as they emerge from sound bases.” (ibid., Ch. 2).  

**Market Conditions Exploited**  
- Bull markets and early-stage recoveries where institutional money flows into accelerating earnings.  
- Structural shift toward “new America” companies with new products, new management, or new industry conditions (“N” in CANSLIM).  
- Liquidity-driven markets in which supply/demand imbalances are visible in price and volume.

**Risk Philosophy & Position Sizing**  
O’Neil recommends 4–8 positions maximum, 20–25% per position at cost, and a strict **7–8% stop-loss** from the purchase price (ibid., Ch. 10). He sells primarily on technical deterioration, not valuation. “You are not right because you bought a stock at a low P/E; you are right only when the market agrees with you.” (Investor’s Business Daily, 1990s editorials).

**What He Looks For**  
- Current quarterly EPS growth ≥ 20–25% (accelerating preferred)  
- Annual EPS growth ≥ 25% over 3–5 years, ROE ≥ 17% (often cited as 15–20%)  
- New price highs after a proper base (cup-with-handle, flat base, etc.)  
- Relative strength line at new highs, stock outperforming the market  
- Institutional sponsorship that is increasing but not excessive (typically 20–80%)

Stocks with superior earnings/sales momentum often get repeatedly re-rated upward by institutions, especially when they are breaking out from multi-month consolidations into new highs.

## B. What market conditions this style exploits

CAN SLIM works best when:
1. **The general market is in an uptrend**
2. **Liquidity is sufficient for institutions to build positions**
3. **Leadership is narrow and identifiable**
4. **Corporate earnings dispersion is high**, allowing true winners to separate from average companies

This style is designed to exploit:
- post-earnings repricing
- breakouts from bases
- momentum persistence
- institutional crowding into quality growth names
- asymmetric upside in “new story” companies

## C. What O’Neil looks for

Documented recurring preferences from *How to Make Money in Stocks*:

- **Strong current quarterly EPS growth**  
- **Strong annual earnings growth history**
- **New products / services / management / industry change / price highs**
- **Limited share supply and evidence of demand**
- **Relative price leadership**
- **Increasing institutional sponsorship, but not over-owned**
- **Buying only when the broad market is supportive**

He also emphasized that the best stocks are often bought **near new highs**, not after large declines. This sharply differentiates him from value investing.

## D. What O’Neil avoids

He consistently warns against:
- buying low-priced “cheap” stocks
- averaging down
- buying laggard stocks
- buying breakouts in weak general markets
- holding losers beyond a small predefined loss
- relying on dividends/book value/low P/E as primary reasons to buy

## E. Risk philosophy and position sizing

O’Neil’s risk control is one of the most documented parts of the method:
- **Cut every loss at no more than 7%–8% below purchase price**  
  Source: *How to Make Money in Stocks*, sell rules chapters.
- Buy only when market conditions are favorable.
- Concentrate capital in strongest ideas rather than broad diversification, but avoid oversized damage through strict stop-losses.

For BigClaw, this implies:
- concentrated growth portfolio is acceptable
- hard stop discipline is essential
- no new buying during non-confirmed market uptrends

## F. Historical performance characteristics

CAN SLIM-style strategies historically show:
- high upside capture in momentum-led bull markets
- sharp relative underperformance if discipline is not applied in bear markets
- higher turnover than quality or value styles
- dependence on trend persistence and earnings momentum
- strong skew: a few large winners drive returns, many small losses are accepted

Academic and practitioner analyses of momentum/growth systems broadly confirm these traits, though exact CAN SLIM replication varies because some components—especially “newness,” base patterns, and true RS rating—are difficult to recreate perfectly from free data.

## G. Behavior by regime

### Bull markets
Best environment. Breakouts succeed more often, institutions chase leaders, earnings revisions expand multiples.

### Bear markets
O’Neil’s method becomes defensive. The **M** factor dominates. New buying should largely stop. Existing positions are often cut by stop-loss or trend failure.

### Sideways/choppy markets
False breakouts increase. Strict stop discipline causes whipsaws. This is a known weakness.

### High-rate environments
Can work if earnings growth is exceptional, but speculative multiple expansion is harder. Leadership narrows toward profitable growth.

### Crisis/liquidity shocks
System de-risks quickly through sell rules. New buys should be highly constrained.

## H. Known weaknesses and blind spots

1. **Whipsaw risk** in range-bound markets
2. Heavy dependence on **accurate market regime detection**
3. Some key CAN SLIM elements are difficult to code with Yahoo data:
   - proper base pattern recognition
   - pivot points
   - follow-through day
   - institutional sponsorship quality
   - “new product / new management”
4. Accounting data timing may be imperfect in Yahoo
5. Relative strength ranking versus the full market is only approximable unless you build your own universe-wide ranking engine

## I. BigClaw design implication

For BigClaw, the portfolio should be defined as:

> A concentrated, trend-following growth portfolio that buys fundamentally superior stocks showing strong recent earnings growth, annual earnings strength, market-leading price action, and proximity to new highs, but only during a favorable broad market trend; it sells quickly on loss control and trend breakdown.
